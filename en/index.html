My approach

How I work

01

Getting to know your company — on your terms

Before I write a single line of code — I learn your processes. The format is flexible: conversations with key people, documentation analysis, workshops — or weeks spent directly on the floor and in the office. I consider the latter most effective, because talking to operators, foremen and engineers gives a picture no documentation can replace. But you decide.

02

Analysis and initial proposals

Based on observations, I identify where time and money are being lost. I propose concrete solutions — not generic off-the-shelf systems, but answers to your real problems.

03

Build and deployment

Delivery time depends on the scale and complexity of the project. Thanks to deep understanding of production processes, it is significantly shorter than with IT companies lacking this experience — I don't waste time learning the industry. Phased deployment so the plant runs without disruptions.

04

Support and development

The system lives with the company. Depending on the billing model — further development as needed or full know-how handover to your IT team.

🔒 NDA included as standard

Every collaboration starts with a mutual non-disclosure agreement. Your procedures, processes and internal data remain trade secrets. I sign and I keep my word.

Building a system takes months.
Understanding production takes years.

Dev companies can build a system in months. But to know what to build — you need to understand production from the inside. Know what frustrates an operator at the end of a shift. What a manager looks for in a morning report. What makes a foreman waste an hour a day on paper summaries.

This combination — deep production knowledge plus IT skills — is not available on the market as a standard offering. The result: systems that technically work, but don't answer the real needs of the plant — and don't deliver the KPIs management actually needs to make decisions.

"You're not dealing with a developer who read about production online. You're dealing with someone who worked thousands of hours in it — and now builds systems for it."

Paper reporting costs money. Hours of transcribing data, human errors, no history, no analysis. Digitalization isn't an expense — it's an investment that often pays off in the first year.
