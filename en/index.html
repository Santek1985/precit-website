Response to inquiry
Sounds familiar?

Does your plant need something different
than what it has today?

01

Several systems, none talking to the other — or barely communicating. ERP, MES, time-tracking, warehouse, HR, quality, safety, helpdesk — each from a different vendor, each with a separate (and far from cheap) license. Integration through Excel and manual re-entry. Each one has its own version of what's happening on the floor.

02

Excel and paper as the glue between systems. Wherever systems don't cooperate, where functions are missing, where something needs to be adapted — Excel steps in. The shift foreman keeps a notebook, the planner a spreadsheet, executives get "summaries" stitched together from three sources. Every summary is different.

03

Machines not connected to the system — data delayed. The production system is there, but older machines aren't integrated. The operator enters counter values manually — when there's time. Data arrives late or with errors. Decisions are made based on the state from an hour ago.

04

Current systems are easy to bypass — and easy to cheat. Designed from an office, not from the shop floor. The operator knows where to skip a step, where to enter a value "from memory" — and the system won't catch it. Worse, the system itself doesn't anticipate situations the designer didn't know about — an unexpected event on the floor has nowhere to be recorded, because there's no such field. You report the missing function to the vendor — and wait months for a decision whether it'll be implemented at all.

05

Every system change is a project with the vendor. You want to add a field, change a report, adjust a workflow, modify the department structure — you hear that it's a project, an estimate, a queue, three months. Six months later the system still doesn't fit, and the contract is already paid.

06

The system looks like every other, not like your company. Vendor logo in the header, product name that has to be explained to employees, terminology from a brochure instead of from your shop floor. The system looks like something you bought — not like something you have.

07

Your internal IT with their hands tied. You hire an IT department with specific technical competencies — but with an external, closed system they can't change anything themselves. Every modification requires the vendor's consultants, their schedule, their estimate. Your engineers can observe, document, escalate — but the actual change is beyond their reach. You're wasting the expertise you have on hand, and paying twice for what's already inside the company.

This isn't a people problem.
It's a tools problem.

The operator enters data unreliably not because they're careless — but because there's no guidance at the right moment for what exactly to do. The shift foreman writes paper reports not because they love paper — but because there's no tool that would fill itself out during the shift. Management doesn't have data not because it's unavailable — but because no one designed how it should reach where it's needed. Each of these problems stems from a tool that wasn't designed for the realities of your shop floor — and from the fact that a typical plant has to juggle four, five, or seven separate systems that were never meant to cooperate.

Typical digitization scenario

What it looks like to build you a system

A production plant, a few dozen people, serial production. A typical situation — no giant IT budget, with a real problem on the floor. Below is the direction we take together.

Before

  • Four or five separate systems, none integrating with another
  • Paper reporting — 2–3 hours of foreman work daily
  • HR tracks medical exams and training, production doesn't know
  • Quality issues caught in the next department or by the customer
  • Production meetings on notebooks, decisions lost between shifts
  • Excel as glue between systems, executives get stitched-together reports

Rollout

  • Process analysis directly on the floor — conversations with operators, foremen, planner
  • Selection of modules that solve the biggest pain today — usually Operations + HR + Quality
  • Configuration of department structure, lines, roles, routings, cards for the specific plant
  • Client branding — logo, colors, system identity
  • Digital meetings, shift handover, automatic notifications
  • Team training, documentation, gradual rollout of further modules

Results

  • One system instead of four to five — end of manual re-entry
  • Reporting time: from 2–3 hours down to 15–20 minutes daily
  • HR and production see the same data — an employee without a current exam doesn't reach the line
  • Quality issues caught during production, not at the customer
  • Decisions from meetings — recorded, visible, with history
  • Executives see live production status without stitching data from three sources
Important note: The data above is representative of a typical digitization scenario at this scale. Every plant is different — that's why I always start from an analysis of the actual process. If a plant isn't ready for automation — the system works just as effectively with manual data entry. Results may vary depending on the process and the scale — but the direction of change remains the same.
